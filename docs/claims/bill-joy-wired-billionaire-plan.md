## Steelman

Bill Joy's essay is real, famous and frightening. A co-founder of Sun
Microsystems, one of Silicon Valley's most respected engineers, wrote in
*Wired* that robotics, genetic engineering and nanotechnology could make
humans unnecessary. The essay does contain a passage about a small elite
controlling the "masses" once machines do all the work, and even reducing
their numbers. If an insider like Joy took that seriously, maybe people
with power discuss such futures more openly than the public knows.

## Short answer

**False.** The article exists, but it doesn't say what Jones says it says.

- **No meeting, no plan.** Joy reports no billionaires' meeting and no agreement to poison or dumb down anyone. The essay grew out of a conversation he had with the inventor Ray Kurzweil at a 1998 conference.
- **The passage about elites isn't Joy's view, or anyone's plan.** It's from the Unabomber Ted Kaczynski's manifesto. Kurzweil quoted it in a book, Joy quoted it from there, and he did so as a warning. Kaczynski was describing what *might* happen, as part of his case against technology.
- **5G didn't exist.** The essay is from April 2000. The first 5G standard was finished in 2018.
- **The essay argues the opposite.** Joy's conclusion is that we should hold back from developing the most dangerous technologies.

## The full picture

### What the essay actually is

*Why the Future Doesn't Need Us* appeared in *Wired* in April 2000. Bill
Joy was then chief scientist at Sun Microsystems, which he co-founded. He
describes how a conversation with Ray Kurzweil, and an early draft of
Kurzweil's book *The Age of Spiritual Machines*, convinced him that
21st-century technologies might escape human control. He argues that
genetics, nanotechnology and robotics are uniquely dangerous because small
groups or even individuals could misuse them, and that the right response
might be **"relinquishment":** deciding not to pursue some research.
Critics called that neo-Luddism. Others took it seriously. Either way,
it's a warning, not a blueprint.

### The passage Jones seems to mean

Early in the essay, Joy quotes a long passage that he first read in
Kurzweil's book. Its author is Theodore Kaczynski, the Unabomber, whose
1995 manifesto it comes from. Paraphrased, it says:

- if machines make all the decisions, we can't predict what happens to us;
- if humans keep control, that control will sit with a small elite, and the masses will no longer be needed;
- such an elite might get rid of most people, or quietly lower the birth rate, or keep everyone comfortable and busy with harmless pastimes, like a good shepherd.

Compare Jones's version: "a billionaire meeting with 200 guys", "the
consensus was", "poison the public", "5G". Every one of those details was
added. Kaczynski's scenario became a plan, the plan was given a meeting,
and the meeting was given a technology that came two decades later.

Joy wrote that he was disturbed to find himself taking the Unabomber's
argument seriously, while rejecting his violence. That discomfort is the
point of the essay.

### Where "a meeting of the elite" might come from

There *is* a well-known story about elites discussing a future where most
people aren't needed, but it isn't in Joy's essay. In their 1996 book *The
Global Trap*, two *Der Spiegel* journalists described the first **State of
the World Forum** (San Francisco, 1995), a gathering of hundreds of
politicians and business leaders. By the authors' account, speakers
predicted a "20:80 society" in which a fifth of the workforce would be
enough to run the world economy. They also say Zbigniew Brzezinski, a former
US National Security Adviser, joked that the rest might be pacified with
"tittytainment": cheap entertainment and food.

Jones's "we're not gonna let people … party all day" and "titrate the dose"
may echo this story. That's my guess, not something he said. Even in the
journalists' telling, though, it's people predicting unemployment, not
agreeing to poison anyone.

### What the claim gets right

- The article, its title and its author are real. Joy did co-found Sun.
- Joy really did think powerful technologies could make humans redundant, and said so in a mainstream magazine.

## Thinking lesson

**Read the whole quote.** Jones named a real article, which makes the claim
sound checkable and therefore trustworthy. But the article, read in full,
contains a *quoted* scenario written by a terrorist, presented as a
warning. Turning it into "the consensus" of "200 billionaires" took three
additions, and none of them is in the text. When someone cites a source,
the question isn't "does it exist?" but "does it say that?"
