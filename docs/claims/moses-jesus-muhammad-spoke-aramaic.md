## Steelman

Judaism, Christianity and Islam are close relatives. Islam explicitly
presents itself as continuing the line of Abraham, Moses and Jesus, whom
the Qur'an honours as prophets. All three come from the same corner of the
world, and their languages (Hebrew, Aramaic, Arabic) are related: the word
for God in each grows from the same old root. Jesus really did speak
Aramaic, the Bible remembers Israel's ancestors as Aramaeans, and the
Qur'an borrows Aramaic religious vocabulary. So, the argument goes, these
aren't three different gods with three different names but one God,
addressed in one family of words, by one chain of prophets.

## Short answer

**The conclusion is a respectable position. The argument for it is mostly
wrong.**

- **Only Jesus spoke Aramaic.** Moses is remembered as a Hebrew, from a time before Aramaic is even attested. Muhammad spoke Arabic.
- **Wrong scriptures.** The Torah is written in Hebrew and the New Testament in Greek. Only a few later chapters of the Bible are in Aramaic.
- **Related words, not the same word.** The Aramaic word for God is *ʾelāhā*. It's a cousin of Arabic *Allāh*, not the same word, and the God of the Torah has his own name, YHWH.
- **The "therefore" doesn't follow.** Whether the three religions worship the same God is a theological question. Many Jews, Christians and Muslims say yes, but because of a shared history and shared beliefs, not a shared word.

## The full picture

### The factual steps, one by one

| He says | Check |
|---|---|
| Jesus spoke Aramaic | **True.** This is the scholarly consensus: Galilean Aramaic was his everyday language. The Gospels keep some of his Aramaic words: *talitha koum* ("little girl, get up", Mark 5:41), *ephphatha* ("be opened", 7:34), *abba* ("father", 14:36), *Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani* ("my God, my God, why have you forsaken me?", 15:34). He probably also read Hebrew, the language of scripture, and may have known some Greek. |
| Moses spoke Aramaic | **No.** The Bible presents Moses as a Hebrew raised at the Egyptian court. Tradition dates him to the 13th century BCE (or the 15th), but Aramaic is first attested in the late 11th century BCE, and its earliest well-dated text is from about 850 BCE. Whether Moses was a historical person at all is debated. Either way, nothing links him to Aramaic. |
| Muhammad spoke Aramaic | **No.** He spoke Arabic, and the Qur'an stresses it: "We have sent it down as an Arabic Qur'an" (12:2), "in a clear Arabic tongue" (26:195). Aramaic, in its Syriac form, was the church language of Arabia's Christian neighbours, and the Qur'an contains Aramaic and Syriac loanwords. That's contact, not a shared language. |
| The Torah was written in Aramaic | **No.** The Torah (Genesis to Deuteronomy) is in Hebrew. Its only Aramaic is two words in Genesis 31:47, where Laban the Aramaean names a pile of stones in his own language while Jacob names it in Hebrew. That shows the Bible treating them as **different** languages. The Aramaic passages of the Bible are in much later books: Ezra (4:8–6:18; 7:12–26), Daniel (2:4b–7:28) and one verse of Jeremiah (10:11). |
| The Bible and all of Jesus' teachings were written in Aramaic | **No.** The New Testament was written in Greek, and nearly all scholars agree on that. Some argue that Aramaic sayings lie behind parts of the Gospels, but the claim that the New Testament was *written* in Aramaic is fringe. The Aramaic (Syriac) Bible, the Peshitta, is a translation from about the 5th century. |
| In Aramaic, "Lord" or "God" is "Allah" | **Partly.** In Aramaic, "God" is *ʾelāh*, or *ʾelāhā* with the article. In Syriac it's *ʾalāhā*, which does sound close to *Allāh*. They're cognates: related words from a shared Semitic root, like Russian *брат* and English *brother*. But "Lord" is a different word, *mārā*, as in *maranatha*, "our Lord, come!". The Hebrew Bible usually says *Elohim*, and God's personal name there is **YHWH**, which sounds nothing like "Allah". |

So of six factual steps, one is true, one is partly true and four are false.

### Where the idea comes from (the kernel)

The mistake is understandable, because there are real links:

- **The languages are relatives.** Hebrew, Aramaic and Arabic are all Semitic, so many basic words look alike. See [The languages of the Bible and the Qur'an](../topics/languages-of-scripture.md).
- **Aramaic was everywhere.** From the 8th to the 4th century BCE it was the common language of the Middle East under the Assyrian, Babylonian and Persian empires. By Jesus' day it had replaced Hebrew as the Jews' everyday language.
- **The Bible remembers Aramaean ancestors.** "A wandering Aramaean was my father" (Deuteronomy 26:5) refers to Jacob, and Abraham's relatives Laban and Rebekah lived in Aram. Even so, Israel's own language was Hebrew: in 2 Kings 18:26 (set around 701 BCE), Jerusalem's officials ask an Assyrian envoy to speak Aramaic so that the ordinary people on the wall *won't* understand.
- **Arab Christians and Jews say "Allah" too.** In Arabic, *Allāh* is simply "God", and Arabic Bibles use it.

### Does a shared word mean a shared God?

No, and not because the answer is "different gods". The argument just
doesn't reach its conclusion:

- **The same word can be used for different ideas of God.** Before Islam, Meccans who worshipped many gods already used *Allāh* for a high creator god among others. The Qur'an itself says so (29:61) and condemns their worship. Same word, very different belief.
- **Different words can name the same God.** A French Catholic says *Dieu*, a Russian one says *Бог*, an English one says *God*. Nobody concludes they have three gods.

What people actually argue about is **history and belief**:

- **The case for "same God":** Islam claims the God of Abraham, Moses and Jesus. The Qur'an tells Muslims to tell Jews and Christians "our God and your God is one" (29:46). Judaism and Christianity share the Hebrew Bible. The Catholic Church's Second Vatican Council said in 1964 that Muslims, "professing to hold the faith of Abraham, along with us adore the one and merciful God" (*Lumen Gentium* 16; see also *Nostra Aetate* 3, 1965). Theologian Miroslav Volf makes a careful Christian case for this view in *Allah: A Christian Response* (2011).
- **The case against, or "not in the same way":** Christians worship God as the Trinity, while the Qur'an rejects the Trinity and the idea that God has a son (4:171, 5:73, 112). So some Christians, and some Muslims, say the two descriptions can't be of the same God. In 2015 Wheaton College, an evangelical college in the US, moved to dismiss professor Larycia Hawkins after she wrote that Christians and Muslims worship the same God. A common middle position: the same God is *intended*, but *described* very differently.

So "the same God" is a real, live question in theology. It's not something
linguistics, or I, can settle, and it isn't what's being fact-checked. What
fails is the specific reasoning offered for it.

### Verdict

**Misleading.** There's a true kernel (Jesus' Aramaic, related words for
God, a shared Abrahamic family), but the claim leaves you believing several
false things: that Moses and Muhammad spoke Aramaic, and that the Torah and
the Gospels were written in it. Confidence is **high** on the language
facts, which are textbook material. The theological conclusion is
deliberately left open.

## Thinking lesson

**Check the "therefore."** An argument can fail in its facts, in its
logic, or both, and the conclusion can still be right for other reasons.
Here the facts mostly fail (two of the three prophets didn't speak
Aramaic), the logic fails (a related word doesn't make the same God), and
the conclusion is a serious position that needs a different argument. When
a conclusion feels warm and generous, it's especially easy to wave the
steps through. See [Check the "therefore"](../thinking/check-the-therefore.md),
and for the true parts underneath, [Kernel of truth](../thinking/kernel-of-truth.md).
