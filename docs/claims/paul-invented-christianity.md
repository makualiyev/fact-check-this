## What was said

In the *Secret History* lectures, Jiang presents the apostle Paul as the
person who popularized, and basically invented, Christianity.

*Wording reconstructed from memory. The episode and timestamp haven't been
located yet, so this checks the claim as remembered, not his full argument.*

## Steelman

Jesus left no writings. The earliest Christian texts we have are Paul's
letters. Paul is the one who took a small Jewish movement to non-Jews,
decided they didn't have to follow Jewish law, and worked out the theology
of salvation through faith in Christ's death and resurrection. Without him
Christianity might have stayed a small Jewish sect. Respected scholars have
called him Christianity's "second founder."

## Short answer

**"Popularized" is fair. "Invented" isn't.** Paul joined the Jesus movement
only a few years after Jesus' death. By his own account he had been
persecuting it. He received its core beliefs from people who were believers
before him, and he had to negotiate with leaders in Jerusalem he didn't
control. What he shaped enormously was *where* the movement went and *how*
its beliefs were explained. The strongest modern scholarship actually makes
Paul *more* Jewish, not the founder of a new religion.

## The full picture

### The timeline leaves little room for invention

| When (approx.) | What |
|---|---|
| 30–33 CE | Jesus is crucified |
| about 33 CE (1–5 years later) | Paul's conversion. Before it, he persecuted the movement |
| about 3 years later | Paul visits Jerusalem for 15 days, meeting Peter (Cephas) and James, the brother of Jesus (Galatians 1:18–19) |
| about 48–50 CE | Jerusalem meeting with the "pillars" James, Peter and John, on whether non-Jews must be circumcised (Galatians 2; Acts 15) |
| about 50–60 CE | Paul's letters, the oldest Christian texts that survive |
| about 66–70 CE | Mark, the earliest Gospel |
| about 62–65 CE | Paul dies in Rome |

### Christianity before Paul, according to Paul

Paul himself is our best witness that he didn't start the movement:

- **He persecuted it first.** "I was violently persecuting the church of God and was trying to destroy it" (Galatians 1:13). You can't persecute something that doesn't exist yet.
- **There were believers and leaders before him.** James, Peter and John led the community in Jerusalem. He mentions Andronicus and Junia, who were "in Christ before I was" (Romans 16:7).
- **He passes on creeds he was taught.** In 1 Corinthians 15:3–7 he writes "I handed on to you … what I in turn had received": that Christ died for our sins, was buried, was raised on the third day, and appeared to Peter, the Twelve and James.
    - "Received" and "handed on" are technical terms for passing on a tradition.
    - Scholars across the spectrum, including skeptics like Bart Ehrman and Gerd Lüdemann, treat this as a creed that predates Paul, dating from within a few years of the crucifixion.
    - The words of the Last Supper (1 Corinthians 11:23–25) are introduced the same way.
- **There are Aramaic leftovers.** He writes *maranatha* ("Our Lord, come!") in Aramaic, untranslated, in a Greek letter (1 Corinthians 16:22). It looks like a prayer from the Aramaic-speaking first believers, who already called Jesus "Lord."

So the core beliefs (Jesus died for sins, rose, appeared, is Lord) were
there before Paul.

### What Paul really did shape (the kernel of truth)

- **The non-Jewish mission without Jewish law.** Paul insisted that non-Jewish converts didn't need circumcision or the food laws. Britannica notes this helped establish Christianity as a separate religion rather than a Jewish sect. His side of the argument eventually won.
- **The writing.** 13 of the New Testament's 27 books carry his name. Scholars agree 7 are certainly his. About half of Acts is about him.
- **The theology.** Justification by faith, being "in Christ," the church as the "body of Christ."
- **The long shadow.** Augustine's conversion and Luther's Reformation both turned on reading Paul's Letter to the Romans.

Britannica calls him arguably the most important figure in Christian
history after Jesus. That's the mainstream claim. "Inventor" isn't.

### Where the "Paul as inventor" idea comes from

The idea has a long history, and Jiang is drawing on it whether he cites it
or not:

- **Thomas Jefferson (1820):** Paul was the "first corrupter of the doctrines of Jesus."
- **Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Antichrist* (1888):** Paul as the "dysangelist," the bringer of bad news who replaced Jesus' message with his own.
- **William Wrede (1904):** Paul as the "second founder of Christianity." Note *second*, after Jesus.
- **Hyam Maccoby, *The Mythmaker* (1986):** the strong version. Paul invented Christianity by mixing Judaism with pagan mystery religions. Mainstream scholars largely reject it.

The mainstream response:

- **David Wenham, *Paul: Follower of Jesus or Founder of Christianity?* (1995):** finds strong continuity between Jesus' teaching and Paul's.
- **The "New Perspective on Paul":** E. P. Sanders (1977), James Dunn (1982), N. T. Wright. They argue Paul has to be read inside the Judaism of his day, not as someone founding a rival to it.
- **The "Paul within Judaism" school:** e.g. Paula Fredriksen, *Paul: The Pagans' Apostle* (2017). Paul lived and died a Torah-observant Jew who believed the end was near and that non-Jews were being brought in as non-Jews. In his lifetime there was no separate religion called "Christianity" for him to found.
- **The "parting of the ways":** Judaism and Christianity separated gradually, over generations after Paul's death, perhaps over centuries.

This is a [consensus and a fringe](../thinking/consensus-vs-fringe.md), not
an even split: Paul's huge role is consensus, and "Paul invented it" is a
minority position.

## Thinking lesson

**Find the kernel of truth, then measure how far the claim stretches it.**
Paul really was enormously important. That's what makes "Paul invented
Christianity" feel right. Strong claims usually grow from a true kernel,
and the useful question is how much the claim adds on top of it. Also
notice the source: an education lecturer restating a 19th-century
provocation (Nietzsche) and a 1980s minority thesis (Maccoby) as if they
were established history. See [Kernel of truth](../thinking/kernel-of-truth.md),
[Consensus vs. fringe](../thinking/consensus-vs-fringe.md), and
[Check the expertise](../thinking/check-the-expertise.md).
