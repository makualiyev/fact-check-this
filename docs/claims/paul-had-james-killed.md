## Steelman

Paul and James's circle really did clash over whether non-Jewish converts
had to keep Jewish law. At Antioch, "certain people came from James" and
the dispute turned bitter (Galatians 2:11–14). And one later Jewish-Christian
text actually shows an "enemy," identified as Saul (Paul's Jewish name),
attacking James in the Temple. So the idea that Paul saw James as a rival
isn't invented from nothing.

## Short answer

**False.**

- **The earliest account names someone else.** Josephus, a Jewish historian writing about 30 years later with no reason to protect Paul, reports that the high priest **Ananus** had James stoned in **62 CE**, while Rome had no governor in Judea.
- **Paul couldn't have done it.** In 62 Paul was a Roman prisoner, held in Caesarea and then Rome since about 57. By 70 he was almost certainly dead.
- **Nobody else blames Paul either.** The 2nd-century Christian account blames "scribes and Pharisees." No ancient source of any date says Paul had James killed.
- **The text that does show "Saul" attacking James** is a late novel, which sets the attack *before* Paul's conversion and has James survive.

## The full picture

### What the sources say about James's death

| Source | Date written | Who killed James, and when |
|---|---|---|
| **Josephus**, *Antiquities* 20.197–203 | about 93 CE | The new high priest **Ananus son of Ananus** took advantage of the gap between Roman governors (Festus had died and Albinus hadn't arrived). He convened a court and had "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ, whose name was James" stoned, with some others. Citizens protested, and Ananus was deposed after three months. **62 CE.** Most scholars accept this passage as genuine. |
| **Hegesippus**, quoted by Eusebius, *Church History* 2.23 | about 170s CE | "The scribes and Pharisees" throw James from the Temple, stone him, and a laundryman finishes him off with a club, and then "immediately Vespasian besieged them." That last link is probably where dates in the late 60s come from. Historians prefer Josephus. |
| **Pseudo-Clementine *Recognitions*** 1.70–71 | 4th-century novel, possibly using a source from about 200 CE | A "hostile man," identified in the text as Saul, starts a riot in the Temple and throws James down the steps. **James survives.** The scene is set *before* Saul's conversion, in the 30s. |

Even the one anti-Paul source puts the attack decades too early and doesn't
end with James's death. Scholars read the *Recognitions* as evidence of how
later Jewish-Christian groups resented Paul, not as history.

### Where Paul was

| Year (approx.) | Paul |
|---|---|
| 57 | Arrested in Jerusalem (Acts 21) |
| 57–59 | Held for two years in Caesarea (Acts 24:27) |
| 60–62 | Under house arrest in Rome (Acts 28:30) |
| **62** | **James is stoned in Jerusalem** |
| 62–67 | Paul dies in Rome |
| 70 | Romans destroy the Temple. Paul almost certainly already dead |

### Rivals, but not enemies to the death

Their relationship had real tension, but also cooperation:

- James, Peter and John gave Paul "the right hand of fellowship" (Galatians 2:9).
- Paul raised money for the Jerusalem community that James led (Romans 15:25–31; 2 Corinthians 8–9).
- In Acts 21, Paul visits James in Jerusalem and follows his advice.

"Who benefits?" can be a useful question, but it is a lead, not evidence.
Here every piece of actual evidence points elsewhere.

## Thinking lesson

**Start with the earliest source, and put the suspect on the timeline.**

- **Start with the earliest source.** The claim skips the earliest account (Josephus, a non-Christian who names the killer) and replaces it with a "who benefits?" guess.
- **Put the suspect on the timeline.** When the event happened, the suspect was in Roman custody hundreds of miles away.
