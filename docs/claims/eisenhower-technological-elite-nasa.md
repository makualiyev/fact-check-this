# Eisenhower's farewell address warned about a "technological elite" because he had rejected NASA's plan for a breakaway civilization

## Steelman

Eisenhower's farewell address is famous for its warning about the
"military-industrial complex," and less famous for a second warning, about
a "scientific-technological elite" capturing public policy. Eisenhower also
really did balk at NASA's plan to land a man on the Moon, and later called
the Moon race "nuts." So a president who knew NASA's plans from the inside
both distrusted its ambitions and warned the nation about elite
scientists. Reading the two together is tempting.

## Short answer

**Misleading.** Both facts are real, but the link between them isn't, and
the "plan" is invented.

- **The warning is real.** On 17 January 1961, Eisenhower said public policy could "become the captive of a scientific-technological elite."
- **It wasn't about NASA.** The passage is about how federal money was changing research: universities depending on government contracts, and experts gaining outsized influence on policy. It doesn't name NASA or space.
- **He didn't oppose NASA. He created it.** He signed the law establishing NASA in 1958.
- **He did say no to the Moon,** in 1960. The reasons on record are cost and his dislike of a "race," not discovering a secret plan.

## The full picture

### What the speech says

The relevant passage, in Eisenhower's words:

> "Partly because of the huge costs involved, a government contract becomes
> virtually a substitute for intellectual curiosity. …The prospect of
> domination of the nation's scholars by Federal employment, project
> allocations, and the power of money is ever present and is gravely to be
> regarded. Yet, in holding scientific research and discovery in respect,
> as we should, we must also be alert to the equal and opposite danger that
> public policy could itself become the captive of a scientific-technological
> elite."

It's a warning in **two directions**: government money might corrupt
science, and scientists might gain too much power over government. The
context is the Cold War arms build-up: huge defence research budgets and
weapons scientists lobbying presidents. Nothing in it is about a hidden
civilization or about space.

### Eisenhower and NASA: the timeline

| When | What happened |
|---|---|
| Oct 1957 | The Soviet Union launches Sputnik. Eisenhower is criticised for falling behind. |
| 29 Jul 1958 | Eisenhower signs the **National Aeronautics and Space Act**, creating NASA as a *civilian* agency. NASA opens on 1 October. |
| May 1960 | NASA's budget request reveals plans for a crewed Moon landing. Eisenhower asks his science advisers to review "the goals, the missions and the costs." |
| Dec 1960 | His advisers estimate a Moon programme at tens of billions of dollars. Eisenhower refuses to commit, reportedly saying he wouldn't "hock his jewels" for it. |
| 17 Jan 1961 | Farewell address. |
| May 1961 | Kennedy commits the US to a Moon landing. |
| 1963 | Eisenhower calls the race to the Moon "nuts." He later dismisses Apollo as a "stunt." |

So the kernel is stronger than it first seems. Eisenhower did "say no to
the NASA plan," if the plan means Apollo. But his reasons are well
documented. He thought a crash programme would be wasteful, he disliked the
idea of a "race," and he wanted a balanced budget. Jones's version adds a
motive for which there's no record at all.

### "NASA is the real government"

This and the "breakaway civilization" come from the conspiracy theory Jones
is setting out. They aren't historical claims that can be checked against
documents, and he cites none. That part is
[unfalsifiable](../thinking/theories-that-cant-lose.md), so the verdict
here is about the checkable part: what the speech says, and why Eisenhower
said no to the Moon.

## Thinking lesson

**Read the whole quote.** "Scientific-technological elite" is a striking
phrase, and out of context it sounds like a hint about something secret.
The paragraph it sits in is about university research grants and expert
lobbying. Then check what the speaker added: "because he said no to the
NASA plan when he found out what it was." That's a second true fact,
attached with a "because" that no document supports.
