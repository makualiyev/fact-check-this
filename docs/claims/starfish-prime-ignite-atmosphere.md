## Steelman

The US really did explode hydrogen bombs in space. The biggest, **Starfish
Prime** in 1962, was 1.4 megatons, about 100 times Hiroshima. It lit up the
Pacific sky, knocked out streetlights in Hawaii and crippled satellites. It
went ahead over the protests of scientists abroad. The project's own
designers didn't fully know what it would do. And the fear that a nuclear
blast might set the atmosphere on fire was real: Manhattan Project
physicists worried about exactly that. If you want an example of governments
gambling with the planet, this is a fair one.

## Short answer

**Misleading.** The tests happened, but the number and the purpose are wrong.

- **How many:** the US carried out **11** high-altitude nuclear tests, in 1958 and 1962. Only a few were in the megaton range. The Soviet Union did about five more. That's nowhere near "over 100."
- **Why:** to study how nuclear blasts in space affect radar, radio, satellites and incoming missiles, and to test an idea for missile defence. Not to ignite the atmosphere.
- **The ignition fear was settled long before.** Physicists raised it in 1942. They concluded before the first test in 1945 that it couldn't happen, and a 1946 report called it "simply impossible."
- **No 1968 test.** The 1963 Partial Test Ban Treaty ended nuclear tests in the atmosphere and in space.

## The full picture

### The real tests

| Year | Series | Shots | What they were for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1958 | Hardtack I (Yucca, Teak, Orange) | 3 | Effects of very high blasts, at the edge of space. Teak and Orange were in the megaton range. |
| 1958 | **Argus** | 3 small (~1.7 kiloton) | Test a theory by physicist Nicholas Christofilos that a blast in space would trap particles in an artificial **radiation belt** that might damage incoming Soviet warheads. It made belts, but they were too weak to matter. |
| 1962 | **Fishbowl** (incl. **Starfish Prime**) | 5 successful | Effects on radar, communications and missile defence, after the Soviet Union ended a testing pause in 1961. |

A **radiation belt** is a zone where Earth's magnetic field traps
high-energy charged particles. The natural ones, the Van Allen belts, had
just been discovered by America's first satellite in 1958.

### What Starfish Prime did

- Exploded on 9 July 1962, about 400 km above Johnston Island in the Pacific, at 1.4 megatons. It was the largest US nuclear test in space.
- It injected enough electrons into Earth's magnetic field to create an **artificial radiation belt** that lasted years.
- Within months, several satellites failed from radiation damage. The best known is **Telstar 1**, the first commercial communications satellite, launched the day after the blast.
- Its **electromagnetic pulse** (EMP), a surge of energy from the blast, disrupted electrical systems in Hawaii, about 1,400 km away.

That's the kernel, and it's genuinely alarming. But the danger was to
satellites and electronics, and the planners knew the sky wouldn't catch
fire.

### The "ignite the atmosphere" fear: when it was, and how it ended

| When | What happened |
|---|---|
| 1942 | Edward Teller raises the possibility that a fission bomb's heat could start a runaway fusion reaction in the air's nitrogen. Hans Bethe's calculations quickly suggest it can't. |
| 1945 | Trinity, the first nuclear test, goes ahead after the question has been examined. |
| 1946 | Report **LA-602**, *Ignition of the Atmosphere with Nuclear Bombs* (Konopinski, Marvin and Teller), concludes that energy is always lost faster than fusion could produce it. |
| 1958–62 | High-altitude tests, more than a decade after the question was closed. |
| 1963 | The Partial Test Ban Treaty bans tests in the atmosphere, in space and underwater. |

Historians note that the 1946 report was a calculation, made without
experimental data on some of the reactions, so the Manhattan Project
physicists couldn't be literally certain. But the worry belongs to 1942–45,
not to the 1960s tests. By then, hundreds of atmospheric tests had already
been carried out.

### Where "over 100" might come from

The US carried out about 200 atmospheric nuclear tests in total, mostly at
or near ground level, over deserts and Pacific atolls. Its 1962 series alone
(Operation Dominic) included more than 30. That's a guess at the source of
the number. Jones gave none. Either way, those weren't "in the upper
atmosphere," and they weren't attempts to ignite it.

## Thinking lesson

**Check the timeline.** "They tested bombs to see if they could ignite the
atmosphere" sounds plausible because both halves are real: the fear
existed, and so did the tests. Putting dates on them breaks the link. The
fear belongs to 1942, it was answered by 1946, and the tests came in 1958
and 1962. The claimed 1968 attempt comes after the treaty that ended such
tests.
