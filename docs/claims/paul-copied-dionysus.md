## Steelman

Early Christians lived surrounded by Greek and Roman religions with
initiations, sacred meals and gods associated with death and new life.
Dionysus was the god of wine, and Christianity's central ritual uses wine.
One Greek myth does tell of the child Dionysus being torn apart and reborn.
A century ago a respected school of German scholars argued that Paul's
sacraments borrowed from these "mystery religions." Today some scholars
still see deliberate Dionysian echoes in the Gospel of John, for example
water turned into wine.

## Short answer

**The parallels are either invented or much weaker than claimed:**

- **No virgin birth.** Dionysus' mother Semele was Zeus' *lover*. His birth story is about sex and a fiery death, after which Zeus carried the unborn child in his thigh.
- **The "killed and reborn" myth is late.** That story (the Orphic "Zagreus" myth) is fragmentary and mostly known from late sources. Its tidy version is partly a modern reconstruction. Rebirth from a saved heart is nothing like a bodily resurrection on the third day.
- **No evidence of eating the god.** There's none that worshippers ate "the body of Dionysus" as communion, and none of human sacrifice in his historical cult.
- **The Eucharist comes from a Jewish meal.** Paul, our earliest source for it, explicitly told Christians they *cannot* share "the table of demons," meaning pagan cult meals.

## The full picture

### The four claimed parallels, one by one

| Jiang says | What the sources show |
|---|---|
| Dionysus was "born of a virgin mother" | **False.** In the standard myth, Semele, a mortal princess of Thebes, is Zeus' lover. Tricked by Hera, she asks to see Zeus in his true form and is burned up by his lightning. Zeus sews the unborn child into his thigh, which is why Dionysus is "twice-born." No mainstream ancient version calls her a virgin. |
| He was "killed by the enemies of Zeus and Zeus… resurrects him" | **A partial echo, from late and patchy sources.** In the Orphic "Zagreus" story the Titans tear apart and eat the infant son of Zeus. Athena saves the heart, and the child is reborn as Dionysus. But the story survives in scattered fragments, and its fullest form comes from a 6th-century-CE writer. The classicist Radcliffe Edmonds (1999) showed that the neat version is partly a modern construction. And rebirth from a heart is not a man returning in his body three days after a public execution. |
| Worshippers ate "the body of Dionysus" to absorb his energy | **No evidence.** Tearing apart and eating raw flesh appears mostly in **myth and drama**. In Euripides' play *The Bacchae*, King Pentheus is torn apart as punishment, not as communion. Historical evidence for real raw-meat rites is thin, involves **animals**, and never describes eating "the god." "Eating the god to absorb him" is a 19th-century scholar's interpretation, not an ancient statement. |
| "The Christians have the same ritual… the Eucharist" | **No.** The earliest account (Paul, 1 Corinthians 11:23–26, about 54 CE) presents it as a tradition he *received* about Jesus' last meal: bread and wine "in remembrance," set in a Jewish meal, which the first three Gospels make a Passover. Paul draws a sharp line: "You cannot drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons… the table of the Lord and the table of demons" (1 Corinthians 10:21). |

Nothing in Paul's letters mentions or shows any interest in Dionysus.

### Human sacrifice and "secret societies still do this"

There is no evidence of human sacrifice in historical Dionysian worship. When
Rome cracked down on the Bacchic cult in 186 BCE, the historian Livy, writing
about two centuries later, reported lurid charges of murder and orgies, and
modern historians treat them as hostile exaggeration. "Secret societies
still do this" comes with no evidence at all and can't be checked.

### Where this idea comes from, and where scholars stand now

- **The "history of religions school"** (around 1900–1930; Wilhelm Bousset, Richard Reitzenstein) argued that Paul's sacraments drew on the Greek mystery religions. It was a real academic position.
- **Later work largely took it apart:**
    - Günter Wagner's study of Paul and the mysteries (1962, English 1967) examined the claimed parallels one by one.
    - Jonathan Z. Smith's *Drudgery Divine* (1990) showed how the comparisons had been driven partly by Protestant anti-Catholic polemic ("Catholic ritual is just paganism"). He criticised both the copying thesis *and* claims that Christianity is entirely "unique."
- **"Dying and rising gods":** Smith argued that this category, popularised by James Frazer's *The Golden Bough*, has no clear example. Tryggve Mettinger (2001) disagrees about some Near Eastern gods, but still finds no evidence that Jesus' story was built from them. Dionysus isn't one of his cases.
- **The live debate today is narrower:** whether the **Gospel of John** (about 90–100 CE, decades after Paul) deliberately echoes Dionysus, for example water into wine at Cana (Dennis MacDonald, *The Dionysian Gospel*, 2017). It's a minority view, and it's about literary echoes, not a copied ritual.

## Thinking lesson

**Parallels aren't proof of copying.** With enough myths to choose from,
you can always find a god who died, a meal with wine, or a special birth.
The test is whether the parallels are **accurate** (Dionysus' "virgin
birth" isn't), **early enough** (the rebirth story is mostly late),
**specific** rather than generic (wine and meals are everywhere), and
whether there's any **sign of contact** (Paul never mentions Dionysus and
condemns pagan cult meals). See [Parallels aren't proof](../thinking/parallels-arent-proof.md).
