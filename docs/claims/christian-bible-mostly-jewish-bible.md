## What was said

In the *Secret History* lectures, Jiang says that roughly 70% of the
Christian Bible is the Jewish Bible. In other words, the Christian story is
largely a Jewish one.

*Wording reconstructed from memory. The episode and timestamp haven't been

## Steelman

Christianity didn't arrive as a self-contained new revelation. Its God, its
prophets, its story of creation, covenant and a promised messiah, and most
of its scripture by volume all come from Judaism. Many Christians mostly
read the New Testament and don't realise how much of their Bible is
inherited.

## Short answer

The number holds up and, by text length, even understates it: the Old
Testament is about **77% of a Protestant Bible**, and more in Catholic and
Orthodox ones. The corrections are about framing. It isn't hidden
knowledge. And although the books are the same, Christians and Jews arrange
and read them differently, so "the Jewish Bible" inside a Christian Bible
is the same text with a different story built around it.

## The full picture

### Same books, different packaging

The Jewish Bible (the **Tanakh**) and the Protestant Old Testament contain
**the same texts**. The book counts differ because of how they're divided:
Jews count Samuel, Kings, Chronicles and Ezra–Nehemiah as one book each,
and the twelve minor prophets as a single book.

| Bible | Old Testament books | Total books |
|---|---|---|
| Jewish Tanakh | 24 | 24 (no New Testament) |
| Protestant | 39 (same content as the Tanakh) | 66 |
| Catholic | 46 (adds 7 "deuterocanonical" books, e.g. Tobit, Judith, 1–2 Maccabees) | 73 |
| Eastern Orthodox | around 49–51 (varies by church) | around 76–78 |
| Ethiopian Orthodox | around 54 (adds e.g. 1 Enoch, Jubilees) | around 81 |

The extra Catholic and Orthodox books are Jewish writings that circulated in
the Greek translation (the **Septuagint**) but never made it into the Jewish
canon. They're Jewish, but they aren't "the Jewish Bible."

### How big is the share? It depends on what you count

This is the place to ask [what the denominator is](../thinking/what-is-the-denominator.md):

| Measure (Protestant Bible) | Old Testament | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Books | 39 of 66 | 59% |
| Chapters | 929 of 1,189 | 78% |
| Verses | 23,145 of 31,102 | 74% |
| Words (KJV) | about 603,000 of about 783,000 | about 77% |

So "~70%" is a fair round number: a bit high if you count books, a bit low
if you count text.

### Same text, different story

- **The order differs, and the order tells a story.**
    - The Tanakh ends with **2 Chronicles**: King Cyrus lets the exiles "go up" to rebuild the Temple in Jerusalem. It ends on return and restoration.
    - The Christian Old Testament follows the Septuagint's order and ends with **Malachi**: God will send Elijah before "the great and terrible day of the Lord." Christians read this as pointing to John the Baptist and Jesus, so the Old Testament flows straight into the Gospels.
- **The reading differs.** Christians read these books as pointing forward to Christ. Judaism reads them through its own tradition of interpretation (Talmud, Midrash) and rejects that reading. Same words, two different traditions.

### The New Testament is a Jewish story too

- **The people.** Jesus, his family, the twelve apostles and Paul were all Jews. Most New Testament authors were Jewish. Luke is traditionally the one non-Jew, though even that is debated.
- **The quotations.** The New Testament quotes the Jewish scriptures constantly: roughly 220–350 explicit quotations depending on how you count, and well over a thousand allusions.

### It isn't a secret, and the church chose it deliberately

Around 140 CE a Christian teacher named **Marcion** argued exactly the
opposite of this claim. He said the God of the Jewish scriptures was a
different, lesser god, and that Christians should drop those scriptures
altogether. He kept only an edited Luke and ten of Paul's letters. The
church excommunicated him (around 144), and writers like Tertullian wrote
long rebuttals. Keeping the Jewish scriptures was a **public, argued-over
decision**, and Marcion's challenge helped push the church to define its
canon. Any introductory textbook on the Bible covers this.

## Thinking lesson

**A true number still needs a denominator.** "70%" could mean 59% or 78%
depending on whether you count books or text. And notice the framing: a
series called *Secret History* presented a fact you can find in any
introductory textbook. When true facts are packaged as revelations, the
packaging lends that "hidden truth" feeling to whatever comes next. See
[Secret framing](../thinking/secret-framing.md) and
[What's the denominator?](../thinking/what-is-the-denominator.md).
