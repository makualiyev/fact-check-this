# Read the whole quote

**The pattern:** the speaker cites something real: an interview, a speech,
a magazine article. The words are often roughly accurate. But the quote is
lifted out of its setting, so a warning becomes a plan, a description of
medical care becomes a confession, or a scenario someone feared becomes
something someone wanted.

Because the quote is real, checking that "he really said that" feels like
checking the claim. It isn't.

**The habit:**

1. **Find the original.** Get the full transcript, article or speech, not a clip or a summary of it.
2. **Read what comes before and after.** Who is speaking, about what situation, and what point are they making?
3. **Ask who is speaking inside the quote.** An author quoting someone in order to argue with them isn't endorsing them.
4. **Check what's been added.** The cited words often carry less than the claim built on them. Compare the two line by line.

**Example:** Alex Jones said Bill Joy's famous *Wired* essay described
billionaires agreeing to dumb down the public. The passage he seems to
mean was written by the Unabomber, and Joy quoted it as a warning. See
[the check](../claims/bill-joy-wired-billionaire-plan.md).
