# Start with the earliest source

**The pattern:** a speaker offers a dramatic version of an event, such as
who killed whom or what someone "really" meant, without mentioning what the
oldest accounts say. Sometimes it rests on a much later text. Sometimes it
rests on nothing but "who benefits?"

**The habit:**

1. **Find the earliest account.** Who wrote it, when, and how close were they to the events?
2. **Note the writer's bias.** It cuts both ways. A hostile or neutral early writer, like a Jewish historian writing about a Christian, is especially valuable when they confirm something.
3. **Treat later sources as later.** A text written centuries afterwards, especially a novel or a polemic, tells you how people *later* remembered things. It can't overrule earlier witnesses without good reason.
4. **Treat "who benefits?" as a lead, not evidence.** Motive suggests where to look. It doesn't show what happened.

**Example:** "Paul had James killed." The earliest account (Josephus,
about 93 CE) names the high priest Ananus. See
[the check](../claims/paul-had-james-killed.md).
