# The languages of the Bible and the Qur'an

## One language family

Hebrew, Aramaic and Arabic are all **Semitic languages**: a family of
related languages from the Middle East, the way Russian, Polish and Czech
are related Slavic languages. They share many roots. The root
*ʾ-l-h* ("god") gives:

| Language | Word for "God" | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hebrew | *ʾel*, *ʾelōah*, and the plural form *ʾelōhīm* | *Elohim* is the usual word for God in the Hebrew Bible. God's personal name there is a different word, **YHWH** |
| Aramaic | *ʾelāh*, with the article *ʾelāhā* ("the God") | Jesus' cry on the cross, *Eloi, Eloi* ("my God, my God"), is Aramaic (Mark 15:34) |
| Syriac (a later Aramaic dialect) | *ʾalāhā* | Still used in the liturgy of Syriac churches |
| Arabic | *Allāh*, most likely from *al-* ("the") + *ʾilāh* ("god") | Arabic-speaking Christians and Jews also call God *Allāh* |

So the words are **cousins**, not one word. Most linguists think *Allāh* is
a native Arabic formation rather than a loan from Aramaic, although some
classical Arab grammarians thought it came from Syriac or Hebrew.

**Greek** (the language of the New Testament) is not Semitic. It belongs to
the Indo-European family, like English and Russian.

## Who wrote and spoke what

| Text or person | Date | Language |
|---|---|---|
| **Aramaic** first appears in inscriptions | late 11th–9th century BCE (the Tell Fekheriye statue, about 850 BCE, is the earliest well-dated text) | Aramaic, the language of the Aramaean kingdoms of Syria |
| **Early Hebrew** inscriptions (e.g. the Gezer calendar) | about 10th century BCE | Hebrew, or a close Canaanite relative |
| **The Hebrew Bible** (Old Testament), including the Torah | written and edited over centuries, roughly 10th–2nd century BCE | **Hebrew**, except Ezra 4:8–6:18 and 7:12–26, Daniel 2:4b–7:28, Jeremiah 10:11 and two words in Genesis 31:47, which are Aramaic |
| Aramaic becomes the region's common language | 8th–4th century BCE (Assyrian, Babylonian and Persian empires) | Aramaic. Hebrew stays the language of scripture and worship |
| **The Septuagint** | from the 3rd century BCE | Greek translation of the Hebrew Bible |
| **Jesus** | died about 30 CE | Mainly **Galilean Aramaic**; probably read Hebrew; maybe some Greek |
| **The New Testament** | about 50–120 CE | **Greek**, with a few Aramaic words left in (*talitha koum*, *abba*, *ephphatha*, *maranatha*) |
| **Targums** | from about the 1st century CE | Aramaic translations of the Hebrew Bible, read in synagogues |
| **Syriac Bible** (Diatessaron, Old Syriac, then the **Peshitta**) | 2nd–5th century CE | Syriac translations made from Greek and Hebrew |
| **Muhammad** and **the Qur'an** | 7th century CE | **Arabic**. The Qur'an calls itself "an Arabic Qur'an" (12:2) |

Some useful terms:

- **Torah:** the first five books of the Bible (Genesis to Deuteronomy), traditionally attributed to Moses.
- **Lingua franca:** a common language used between people with different native languages. Aramaic played that role from Egypt to Iran for centuries, as Greek did later.
- **Loanword:** a word borrowed from another language. The Qur'an has a number of Aramaic and Syriac loanwords, mostly religious terms, which shows contact with Jewish and Christian communities, not that it was written in Aramaic.

## Where scholars disagree

- **How much Hebrew and Greek Jesus used.** Almost everyone agrees Aramaic was his everyday language. How well he knew Hebrew and Greek is debated.
- **Aramaic behind the Gospels.** Some scholars look for Aramaic sayings behind the Greek Gospels. The claim that the New Testament was *written* in Aramaic ("Aramaic primacy") is a fringe view.
- **Aramaic in the Qur'an.** Everyone accepts Aramaic loanwords. The claim that the Qur'an is largely a Syro-Aramaic text ("Christoph Luxenberg", 2000) has found only limited acceptance.
